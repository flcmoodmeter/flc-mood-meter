Global Parameters

Turns a stretch of responses into sound. Each step is one hour (within a chosen day) or one day (within a chosen range); whichever quadrant dominates a step picks a scale, how energetic/pleasant those responses were shapes the note, and how many there were shapes loudness. Uses the sidebar filters (location, energy, pleasantness, etc.) as the underlying data. Switch Scale palette to "Quadrant grid" to instead follow a single quadrant's own 25 buttons: each step's pitch is set by exactly where its responses sit on that quadrant's own 5×5 grid (row = how intense the energy, column = how intense the pleasantness/unpleasantness), spanning two octaves plus one note, and note length shortens as intensity rises for an audible tempo cue. Choose "Mood (major → minor)" to hear pleasantness directly: pleasant steps stay major, unpleasant ones pick up minor and clashing notes. Collapse this section with its triangle once it's set up, to keep it out of the way of the tracks below.

–

Each quadrant gets its own track: a channel strip on the left and its own lane on the right showing when that quadrant is sounding. Click a strip's triangle to collapse its controls (and shrink its lane) out of the way. Octave shifts each quadrant's notes up or down from wherever they're already pitched — 0 is no shift (roughly C4). Pan sets each quadrant's fixed left/right position. Reverb send controls how much of that quadrant reaches the shared reverb (100% = the master Reverb Amount above, 0% = none) — it only does anything once Reverb is switched on. Noise blends in a hiss under each quadrant's tone, from Off (pure tone) up to fully noise-dominant, useful for marking a quadrant as "noisier" or more chaotic-sounding. Glide is portamento — instead of jumping straight to a note's pitch, it slides up or down from the previous note that same quadrant played, from Off (instant, the old default) up to a slow, singing slide between consecutive notes; the Tails-across-gaps toggle above controls whether that glide survives a rest.

Drum Parameters

Below the quadrant tracks sit four more: an approximate, synthesized kick/hi-hat/snare, plus a 4th accent track that's either a Clap or a Clave — a toggle under its own triangle switches between the two — that loops under the reel. At 1x, each hour/day step in the timeline is one 16th-note of the pattern — so 16 timeline steps make one measure, and the backbeat's pulse is exactly the reel's own step tempo, speeding up or slowing down automatically with Tempo and Playback speed. Speed (in the bar above the drum tracks) doubles or halves that ratio (2x packs two 16th-notes into one timeline step; ½x stretches one 16th-note across two, for a halftime feel) without changing the reel's own tempo. Click a cell in a drum lane to arm that drum on that step; any combination, or none. Each track's own Fill/Rand/Clear (under its triangle) act on that row alone; Fill All/Randomize All/Clear All act on all four at once.

Exclude suspicious entries

When enabled, entries that appear to be spam, pranks, or accidental taps are excluded from all charts and counts.

Detection rules

  • 🚀 Speed — Response selected in under 2 seconds (faster than a person can meaningfully read and choose)
  • 🔁 Repeat — Same emotion chosen 3 or more times in a row within a single session
  • ⚡ Burst — 5 or more responses submitted within 60 seconds in a single session

Flagged entries are never deleted — turn this filter off to include them again.

Emotional center of gravity

Tracks where the average mood sits, period by period, and how that average has moved over time.

How it works

  • The mood grid has two axes: horizontal is pleasantness (unpleasant ← → pleasant), vertical is energy (low ← → high). Each quadrant corresponds to one of the four quadrant colors used throughout the dashboard.
  • For each time period (day/week/month, based on the selector in this card's header), every response in that period is scored on each axis from -1 to +1 based on exactly which emotion word was chosen: the sign says which side (Pleasant is positive, Unpleasant negative; High energy positive, Low negative), and the size says how far toward the edge of the grid the word sits. A word at the outer edge scores close to ±1, while one near the middle of the grid scores closer to 0. Those scores are then averaged, which gives one point — the “centroid,” or center of mass — representing where that period's responses clustered on average.
  • Each point is also matched to the closest emotion word on the grid, shown in large type beside the grid and in each dot's hover tooltip. Because an average can land between words, it is shown with how tightly that period's responses cluster (tight cluster, mixed, or widely mixed) and, when different, the word chosen most often. A “mixed” label means the closest word is a summary, not something many people actually picked. Below it, two bold arrows (energy and pleasantness) show whether this period is up, down or steady compared with the period before it, with the size of the change. The arrow tilts to match: pointing right when steady, angled up-right or down-right for a modest change, and straight up or down for a large one. A small, fainter copy to its left shows the previous step’s arrow.
  • Those points are plotted on the grid and connected in chronological order by a line, so you can trace the path the average mood has taken over time.
  • The first point is hollow (outlined only) and the last point is solid and larger, so you can tell at a glance which end is the start and which is “now,” and see which direction the path is heading.

What it's good for: if the line is drifting toward the upper-right (High Energy, Pleasant), the overall mood is trending positive and energized. If it's crawling toward the lower-left (Low Energy, Unpleasant), that's a trend worth a closer look. It's a compact way to see direction of travel, which the “Mood trajectory” line chart above it shows as two separate numeric trends (pleasantness and energy) rather than a single combined path.
